# Verify every speaker fact before publishing it

The site should build speaker extras the way a careful fact-checker would. Start from the identity it already has (Sessionboard name, title, company, photo and LinkedIn URL). Collect candidate facts from third-party editorial pages, employer pages, the speaker's own channels and podcast indexes. Publish a fact only when a human has confirmed that its linked source is about this speaker and says what the profile claims. Few APIs survive scrutiny for that work. Wikidata, Podcast Index, Apple's iTunes Search and a capped YouTube search are free and fit a static build. Crunchbase, LinkedIn, NewsAPI, Listen Notes' free tier and people-data brokers are closed, too expensive or contractually off-limits. The biggest practical risk is not the law but mistaken identity: AI research tools blend same-named people into one biography and cite broken or fabricated URLs. Every claim therefore needs a verbatim quote, a fetched URL and two independent identity signals. Legal exposure for a free, ad-free community planner is low but not zero. Texas applies a negligence standard to defamation of private figures, and copyright in headshots usually belongs to photographers. So the site should keep to professional facts, link rather than copy, never sell the data, and remove anything on request within 48 hours. Expect many of the ~273 profiles to end up with no researched extras at all, and treat that as a correct outcome rather than a gap to fill.

## Third-party editorial sources outrank the databases people assume are authoritative

Rank sources by independence from the speaker, then by how directly they state the fact. Wikipedia's biography-of-living-persons policy is a good model. A person's own publications may be used about that person only if the material "is not unduly self-serving" and "does not involve claims about third parties." Self-published sources should never be used as third-party sources about other living people ([WP:BLP](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP:BLP)). That rule places news coverage, award lists, other conferences' speaker pages and host-written podcast show notes at the top. Employer leadership pages and press releases come next, and they are the best source for a current title. The speaker's personal site, Substack, YouTube channel or GitHub follow. These are fine for uncontroversial facts about themselves and are the natural home of the "personal website" field. The Sessionboard bio and LinkedIn link belong in this third tier: they are reliable for what speakers say about themselves, not for claims about anyone else.

The databases many people treat as authoritative sit below all of these. **Crunchbase is largely self-reported**, with accuracy that "varies by company and by field" ([FullEnrich](https://fullenrich.com/tools/What-is-Crunchbase), a competing vendor), and users can edit profiles themselves ([Crunchbase support](https://support.crunchbase.com/hc/en-us/articles/115011260487-What-are-the-guidelines-for-adding-content-to-Crunchbase)). Bloomberg reported that startups inflate or invent figures in Crunchbase, CB Insights and PitchBook ([Bloomberg, 2022](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-02-07/startup-data-integrity-vexes-crunchbase-cb-insights-pitchbook)). Google Scholar's automatic author attribution misassigns papers between people who share common surnames, and that has skewed citation leaderboards as recently as December 2025 ([Times Higher Education](https://www.stg.timeshighereducation.com/node/740547)). ORCID is more useful because it marks "validated assertions" from trusted institutions, such as a university confirming employment, separately from claims people make about themselves ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/orcid-trust-markers)). These sources are useful for finding leads. A lead becomes publishable only after a higher-tier source confirms it.

AfroTech's own editorial output is a high-value tier-one source for this population. The **AFROTECH Future 50** has run every year since 2022, and the 2026 list names 50 honorees in five categories ([AfroTech](https://afrotech.com/meet-future-50)). It is nomination-based ([AfroTech](https://afrotech.com/listicle/2024-afrotech-future-50-submit-to-be-considered/)), so the site should cite it as "named to the 2026 AFROTECH Future 50," not as proof of anything wider. Podcasts need dedicated indexes, because general web search misses episodes where a guest is named only in show notes or transcripts. Listen Notes searches transcripts, and Podchaser tracks credited guest appearances ([BlitzMetrics](https://blitzmetrics.com/how-we-use-listen-notes-to-find-track-and-repurpose-every-podcast-appearance/)). Podcast Index can search on the Podcasting 2.0 `<podcast:person>` tag, which records real credits rather than passing mentions, but only a minority of shows publish that tag ([Glama: podcastindex-mcp](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/conorbronsdon/podcastindex-mcp/tools/search_by_person)).

Wikipedia will cover only a small minority of these speakers, and missing pages say little about prominence. Fewer than 19% of English Wikipedia's biographies of writers, inventors and academics are about women ([UNC CITAP](https://citap.unc.edu/publications/ms-categorized-wikipedia/)). Women and people of color are more likely to have their pages deleted, even with equal or greater online presence than white male peers ([CITAP](https://citap.unc.edu/publication-tag/wikipedia)). Clarice Phelps, likely the first African American woman to help discover a chemical element, had her page deleted after a notability dispute ([Undark](https://undark.org/2019/04/25/wikipedia-diversity-problem/)). The site should never use Wikipedia presence as a filter. Where a page exists, cite the source Wikipedia cites, not Wikipedia itself.

Fun facts need the strictest criteria, because no platform the research could verify publishes editorial ones (see the platforms section). A usable fun fact has a named publisher other than the speaker, or is the speaker's own words in a third-party venue such as a podcast interview. It also carries a date and states the fact directly. Safe categories are awards and lists with the year, books, patents, products shipped, companies or nonprofits founded, a career pivot told by the speaker in an interview, side projects, causes the speaker champions publicly, and notable talks. Net worth, age, family, health, religion and politics are out. So is anything found only on a bio farm. One writer found an AI-generated site calling her a millionaire, which she "is not and has never been," and she warns that sites claiming facts about "birthdays, astrology, or net worth" are often wrong ([Diana Lang, 2025](https://dianalangpoetry.com/2025/09/13/you-cannot-trust-the-robots-to-tell-the-truth/)). People-search and contact brokers belong on a hard blocklist. Opt-out services report that brokers such as RocketReach and ZoomInfo collect data without verifying it and republish profiles after opt-outs ([DeleteMe](https://joindeleteme.com/blog/how-to-remove-yourself-from-zoominfo/)), though DeleteMe sells removals and is an interested source.

## Only four programmatic sources fit a free, static build

The API landscape shrank sharply in 2025–26, and most of what remains either costs too much or forbids storing results on a public site. The table summarises what the research found. Several terms could be checked only through secondary sources, because the primary documentation pages were blocked during research. Check them before building.

| Source | Access and cost | Fit for this site | Key constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wikidata / Wikipedia | Free, no key; query service has a 60-second limit per query and 60 s of processing per minute per client ([WikidataQueryServiceR](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/packages/WikidataQueryServiceR/WikidataQueryServiceR.pdf)) | **Good** for identity links and official websites; low coverage | Wikipedia text needs CC BY-SA attribution; Wikidata's CC0 status is unverified here |
| Podcast Index | Free key and secret, no approval needed ([podcast-index-api](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/podcast-index-api@2.0.0/README.md)) | **Good** for guest credits via person tags | Formal terms and rate limits unverified |
| Apple iTunes Search | No key, about 20 calls per minute; Apple recommends caching ([Apple](https://performance-partners.apple.com/resources/documentation/itunes-store-web-service-search-api/)) | **Fair**, mainly for finding podcasts a speaker hosts | Searches show metadata, not guests |
| YouTube Data API v3 | Free, 10,000 units a day; search is capped (about 100 searches a day) ([Google](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/getting-started)) | **Fair** for talks, at about 3 days for 273 speakers | Stored API data reportedly must be refreshed or deleted within 30 days ([ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-data-api/)) |
| GDELT DOC 2.0 | Free, no key, about 1 request every 5 seconds ([finance-query client](https://docs.rs/crate/finance-query/3.0.0/source/src/adapters/gdelt/client.rs)) | **Fair** for news leads | Noisy name matches; returns URLs and titles only |
| Brave Search API | About $5 of monthly credit (~1,000 queries), card required ([apiserpent](https://apiserpent.com/blog/free-brave-search-api)) | **Fair** for leads on personal sites and talks | Storage rights for results unverified |
| Listen Notes | Free tier: 300 requests a month; server-side caching allowed only on Enterprise ([Listen Notes](https://www.listennotes.com/api/pricing/)) | **Poor** for automation; use the website by hand | Caching terms rule out committing results |
| NewsAPI.org | Free plan "cannot be used in a staging or production environment" ([NewsAPI](https://newsapi.org/pricing)) | **Unusable** | Paid plan reportedly ~$449 a month |
| Google Custom Search, Bing | Bing retired Aug 11, 2025 ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/announcements/bing-search-api-retirement)); Google closed to new customers, ending Jan 1, 2027 ([Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/google-search-json-api-alternative)) | **Unavailable** | — |
| Crunchbase API | Person data needs an Enterprise licence, reportedly $50,000+ a year ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/crunchbase)) | **Unusable** | Redistribution on a public site unlikely to be licensed |
| LinkedIn | No self-serve lookup of other members | **Link only** | Scraping is a live contract risk (below) |
| People Data Labs and similar | Registered data broker ([California AG](https://oag.ca.gov/data-broker/registration/190662)) | **Excluded** | Conflicts with the site's rule against publishing personal data |

Wikidata earns its place through one join, not through coverage. Its property P6634 stores a person's LinkedIn profile ID ([Bioregistry](https://bioregistry.io/linkedin)). The site already holds each speaker's LinkedIn URL, so a single batched SPARQL query over all 273 slugs finds the speakers who have Wikidata items without any name search. Those items then supply official websites and other identifiers. A name search, by contrast, returns many false positives for common names. Any hit from one is only a candidate for human review.

LinkedIn should stay a link and a join key, never a data source. In *hiQ v. LinkedIn* the court found hiQ in breach of LinkedIn's User Agreement. The case ended in a $500,000 judgment, a permanent injunction and an order to delete the scraped data ([National Law Review](https://www.natlawreview.com/article/linkedin-s-data-scraping-battle-hiq-labs-ends-proposed-judgment)). LinkedIn then sued Proxycurl, which shut down around July 2025 ([Nubela](https://nubela.co/blog/goodbye-proxycurl/)), and sued ProAPIs in October 2025 ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/linkedins-war-against-bot-scrapers-ramps-up-as-ai-gets-smarter)). "LinkedIn API alternative" vendors in 2026 most likely depend on the same scraping LinkedIn is litigating.

Across every search API, the safe pattern is the same. Search results are leads for a reviewer, and the site publishes only the URL, title, outlet and date the reviewer chose. It never publishes snippets, thumbnails or statistics. That pattern also sidesteps most caching and storage restrictions, because the committed data is the site's own curated list of links rather than stored API output.

## A name match is worth nothing without a second identifier

Mistaken identity is the most likely way for this project to harm someone. Thousands of people can share a given name and family name ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/wp/?p=3238)). ORCID itself advises organisations not to attach identifiers on the strength of a name search alone ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/author-name-disambiguation-orcid-algorithmic-matching-cris)). Classic record linkage gives a workable model. Fellegi–Sunter scoring adds weighted evidence across fields and gives more weight to agreement on rare values than on common ones. It then uses two thresholds to sort pairs into match, non-match and a "clerical review" zone ([Winkler, US Census](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/1999/adrm/rr99-04.pdf)). At 273 speakers that should be a checklist, not a fitted model.

The strongest signal is a self-asserted link. When the speaker's LinkedIn or personal site links to a page, or the page links back, that is the same reciprocal-link logic behind IndieWeb `rel="me"` and Mastodon verification ([Eric Meyer](https://meyerweb.com/eric/thoughts/2022/11/30/how-to-verify-site-ownership-on-mastodon-profiles/)). The next strongest is a shared persistent ID, such as a Wikidata item carrying the same LinkedIn ID. After those come the employer and title appearing together in the same dated source, and distinctive bio facts such as a named former employer or book title. Conference co-occurrence and a by-eye headshot comparison only support a match. The table turns this into a scoring rule. The point values are the research team's own heuristic, not figures from the literature.

| Evidence in a candidate source | Points |
|---|---|
| Self-asserted link, or shared persistent ID | +3 |
| Employer and title together, with dates consistent with LinkedIn | +2 |
| Distinctive bio fact matches | +2 |
| Matching region, field, conference co-occurrence or headshot resemblance | +1 each |
| Any hard contradiction (different employer at the same date, different field, clearly different photo) | −3 |
| Name agreement | 0 |

A common name should require at least one +3 or two +2 signals. Each speaker record should also carry a one-line "disambiguating description" (title, company, city), in the spirit of Wikidata's rule that no two items can share both a label and a description ([Wikidata tutorial](https://wdqs-tutorial.toolforge.org/index.php/simple-queries/the-simplest-query/adding-description/)). A "known not this person" list stops reruns from re-adding a rejected match. Wikidata editors moved to this deprecate-rather-than-delete pattern after deleted identifiers kept reappearing ([Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/User_talk:Arcadialib)). Face-search engines such as PimEyes are out. Privacy groups call them "deeply privacy-intrusive" and a tool for stalkers ([Big Brother Watch](https://bigbrotherwatch.org.uk/2022/11/pimeyes-a-facial-search-engine-could-be-used-by-stalkers/)).

Name variation matters for this population. Researchers should expect hyphenated and double-barrelled surnames, married-name changes, nicknames, initials, West African and Caribbean naming orders, and transliteration variants ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/games-diversions/9781449398651/ch21.html)). Generate variants on purpose, but count a variant match only alongside employer or bio corroboration. Use a former name only when the speaker publishes it. Never "correct" the spelling, diacritics or capitalisation the speaker gave AfroTech. The research found no sources on naming conventions specific to Black and African-diaspora tech communities, so this guidance is inferred from general name-matching work.

If the site uses AI agents to gather candidates, it must assume their output is wrong until a script and a human prove otherwise. The Tow Center tested eight AI search tools with 1,600 queries and found them wrong more than 60% of the time. More than half of Gemini's and Grok 3's citations led to fabricated or broken URLs, and nearly every tool preferred a wrong answer to admitting a limitation ([CJR](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-theyre-all-bad-at-citing-news.php)). A Nature Communications study found 50–90% of LLM responses not fully supported by their own citations ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12003634/)). Most relevant here, the AmbigBio benchmark showed retrieval-augmented models blending facts about different same-named people into one plausible biography ([arXiv 2402.05629](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05629v4)). A January 2026 benchmark of deep-research agents found none robustly reliable, with early fabrications cascading through later steps ([arXiv 2601.22984](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22984)). Because these tools rarely abstain, the agent prompt must treat "nothing found" as a normal, expected answer.

## Legal exposure stays low only while the site is non-commercial, linked and correctable

**Right of publicity.** Texas misappropriation requires using a name or likeness for its value, "not in an incidental manner or for a newsworthy purpose" ([Matthews v. Wozencraft, 5th Cir. 1994](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/015/15.F3d.432.93-4434.html)). Newsworthiness is applied generously but does not cover advertising ([DMLP](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/texas-right-publicity-law)). The Restatement adds that "a plaintiff's name is not appropriated by mere mention of it" ([Harvard Berkman](https://cyber.harvard.edu/privacy/Privacy_R2d_Torts_Sections.htm)). Free, ad-free profiles of announced speakers, tied to their public sessions, sit at the low-risk end. Risk rises with sponsors or ads beside profiles, social cards that imply endorsement, or selling the data. In *Yeager v. Cingular*, use tied to the defendant's marketing was held not incidental ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2010/01/yeager-v-cingular-wireless-llc-et-al)). Fun facts that drift into private life weaken the newsworthiness argument, because they no longer relate to the public activity.

**Copyright in bios and photos.** Headshots usually belong to the photographer, and clients receive only "a limited license to use the image" ([Businesses Grow](https://businessesgrow.com/2015/04/08/event-photos-law)). Typical speaker agreements grant rights to the organiser, not to third parties ([Euromoney terms](https://euromoney.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Speaker-Agreement-Standard-Terms.pdf)). Embedding an image hosted elsewhere is protected by the Ninth Circuit's server test ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://dwt.com/insights/2023/08/server-test-instagram-case-ninth-circuit)). That test is not settled in the Fifth Circuit, which covers Texas ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/copyright/1805848/is-embedding-someone-elses-image-copyright-infringement-the-fifth-circuit-may-finally-tell-us)), and copying images to your own server weakens it ([Loeb & Loeb: Evox](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2022/12/evox-productions-llc-v-verizon-media-inc)). This creates a real tension with performance advice, since a self-hosted photo loads reliably on convention Wi-Fi. The cleanest fix is to ask AfroTech for permission. Without it, hotlink with credit or show an initials avatar. Facts in a bio are free to use, but its wording is protected expression ([LMU](https://library.lmunet.edu/ILT/copyright_fairuse)). Quote the official bio with credit and write any "in brief" summary in the site's own words. Researched articles and talks should appear as title, outlet, date and link, never as copied excerpts or thumbnails.

**Defamation.** A private-figure plaintiff in Texas needs to show only negligence ([Nolo](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/how-long-do-i-have-to-file-a-defamation-lawsuit-in-texas.html)), and most speakers are likely private or limited-purpose public figures. A wrong "fun fact" attributed through a name mix-up is the classic exposure. Texas's Defamation Mitigation Act helps a careful publisher. A plaintiff must first make a timely written request for correction ([HB 1759 analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/83R/analysis/html/HB01759E.htm)), and the Texas Supreme Court treats that requirement as mandatory ([Hogan v. Zoanni](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1452311/180944d.pdf)). A visible correction channel and fast turnaround fit the Act's design, and sourced, dated claims are the best evidence against negligence.

**Privacy law.** CCPA covers for-profit businesses above revenue thresholds and excludes publicly available information ([CPPA FAQ](https://cppa.ca.gov/faq.html)). Texas's TDPSA exempts SBA-defined small businesses and also excludes publicly available data ([O'Melveny](https://www.omm.com/insights/alerts-publications/signed-texas-added-to-the-list-of-states-enacting-a-comprehensive-privacy-law/); [Baker Hostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/privacy-deep-in-the-heart-of-texas-an-overview-of-the-texas-data-privacy-and-security-act)). The Texas data-broker law, expanded in September 2025, targets businesses handling data they did not collect from the person directly ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250904-texas-expands-and-modifies-data-broker-registration-law)). Selling or licensing the compiled profiles is the act most likely to bring a non-commercial site into scope. GDPR probably does not reach a US site that Europeans can merely access, because Article 3(2) requires targeting ([Faegre Drinker on EDPB guidance](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2018/12/in-or-out-edpb-publishes-much-awaited-guidance-on-gdprs-territorial-scope)). Behaving as if legitimate interests applied costs little. That means minimal role-related data, a public notice explaining sources and purpose, and honouring every objection by removal, which goes beyond Article 21's requirement ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-object)). None of this is legal advice, and the research could not read several primary statutes directly.

**Sensitive data and corrections.** Never publish health, religion, politics, sexual orientation, immigration status, criminal history, family, home location, age or finances, even when the speaker has posted them elsewhere. The SPJ Code holds that "private people have a greater right to control information about themselves than public figures" ([SPJ](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~jour/spj/SPJcode.html)). For disputes, adopt Wikipedia's rule. Unsourced or poorly sourced material about a living person "must be removed immediately," and the burden of proof rests on whoever adds it ([WP:BLP](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP:BLP)). The site should remove first and discuss later. It should act within 48 hours, the same day during conference week (a proposed target, not a legal standard). It should also keep a suppression list that the build scripts read, so a regenerated `data/` never brings a removed field back.

## Platforms let speakers write their own profiles, so editorial extras need visible labels

Every platform the research could verify converges on the same profile shape: photo, name, tagline or title plus company, bio, a list of links, and sessions. Sessionize's API speaker object carries `tagLine`, `bio`, `profilePicture`, `links` and `questionAnswers` ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/connectors/sessionizeip/)). Swapcard caps bios at 1,000 characters and keeps contact details private until a connection is made ([Swapcard](https://help-attendees.swapcard.com/en/articles/8185450-what-information-in-my-profile-is-accessible-by-other-participants)). Eventbrite recommends a 40–60 word short bio and a 60–160 word long bio ([Eventbrite](https://www.eventbrite.com/blog/speaker-bio-ds00/)). Personal touches, where they exist, are speaker-supplied answers to organiser questions. Examples are Sessionize's `questionAnswers` and Whova's up to five custom fields, each set to public or internal ([Whova](https://whova.com/blog/speaker-form-custom-fields/)). The research found **no platform publishing third-party-sourced fun facts**. This site would be breaking new ground, so its researched layer must be visibly separate, labelled as community-compiled and sourced line by line. Speakers own and edit their profiles everywhere else ([Sessionboard](https://www.sessionboard.com/blog/7-new-updates-to-enhance-the-speaker-experience-save-time-and-improve-speaker-management)). A no-sign-in community site has to replace that control with a prominent correction link.

Presentation should follow from those findings. Show the official bio verbatim with "From the AfroTech 2026 official schedule." Add an "in brief" line of about 160 characters that doubles as the meta description. Put the full bio behind a `<details>` element on mobile. Researched items go below as short, dated, linked lines under a heading such as "From public sources." Use text-labelled links ("LinkedIn," "Website") rather than icon-only ones. Emit a JSON-LD `Person` with `sameAs` links, referenced from each session's `Event` through `performer`, because schema.org's Event has no `speaker` property ([schema.org](https://schema.org/Event); [Yoast](https://developer.yoast.com/features/schema/pieces/event)). With about 300 speakers, a single static directory page with client-side search and a track filter is enough. Copy Whova's "incomplete profile" filter ([Whova](https://whova.com/speaker-management-software/)) as a build-time report listing speakers with missing photos, bios or sources.

## Recommended pipeline and source priority

The pipeline below fits the repo's stdlib-Python, generated-data model. Its one new hand-maintained input is a reviewed-facts file plus a suppression list under `data/sources/`, which the build reads and never overwrites.

| Step | What happens | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Seed identity | From Sessionboard: name, title, company, LinkedIn slug, photo URL, bio; generate name variants and a disambiguating description | One identity record per speaker |
| 2. Structured joins | One batched Wikidata query on P6634 LinkedIn IDs; Podcast Index person search; iTunes search for hosted shows | Candidate websites, IDs, podcast credits |
| 3. Targeted search | Per speaker: `"Name" "Company"` on employer site, AfroTech/Blavity, news, `site:youtube.com`, Substack/Medium, award lists; Listen Notes by hand; GDELT and Brave for leads | Candidate URLs (never published directly) |
| 4. Evidence capture | Each candidate claim stored as `{text, url, publisher, date, tier, identity_quote, claim_quote}`; one speaker per agent session | Evidence rows |
| 5. Mechanical check | Script fetches each URL, requires HTTP 200 with no homepage redirect, confirms both quotes appear; blocklisted domains rejected | Pass/fail per row |
| 6. Human review | Score identity; publish only **high**; hold **medium**; discard **low** and log the URL as "not this person" | Reviewed facts file |
| 7. Publish | Build merges reviewed facts, applies the suppression list, renders the profile with per-line sources and "Last checked" date | Profile pages |
| 8. Maintain | Correction link on every page; removal within 48 hours; re-check links every 30 days; retire researched fields 30–90 days after the event | Corrections log in git |

Source priority, highest first: (1) the AfroTech 2026 official schedule for name, title, company, bio, photo and LinkedIn, authoritative for the current role; (2) third-party editorial pages such as AfroTech Future 50 and other award lists, news, other conferences' speaker pages and host-written podcast notes; (3) employer leadership pages and press releases; (4) the speaker's own site, newsletter, YouTube channel or GitHub, for self-facts and the website field, published only when linked from or back to their LinkedIn or Sessionboard profile; (5) Wikidata items reached through the LinkedIn ID join, for identifiers and websites; (6) Crunchbase, Google Scholar and unvalidated ORCID, as leads only; and never people-search sites, contact brokers, bio or net-worth farms, scraped LinkedIn mirrors, or uncited AI answers. Before scaling to all 273, run a pilot on 20 random speakers. The research found no coverage statistics for this population, so the pilot is the only way to learn how many profiles will gain anything and how much review time each one costs.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "where can we find more about each speaker?" to "what can we prove about each speaker?" The open web holds plenty of material, but a meaningful share of it is about someone else with the same name, invented by a bio farm, or cited wrongly by an AI tool. The limiting factor is reviewer time per verified fact, not data access. That makes a sparse profile an honest result rather than a failure. It also means the useful investment is in the evidence schema, the URL-and-quote checker and the suppression list, not in more APIs.

The second implication is that the site's protections and its credibility come from the same features. Per-line source links, dated claims, a visible correction path and fast removal are what the Texas Defamation Mitigation Act rewards, and they show the reasonable care that answers a negligence claim. They also give speakers the control that platforms like Sessionize hand them through accounts. One question remains open and is worth a single email: whether AfroTech will permit reuse of headshots and bios. A yes would settle the copyright question and allow fast, self-hosted images. A no would mean hotlinking or initials, which the site should be ready to use either way.
