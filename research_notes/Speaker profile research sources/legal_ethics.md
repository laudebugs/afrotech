# Legal and ethical guardrails for publishing speaker profiles (AfroTech 2026 planner)

> **Informational only, not legal advice.** These notes cover US law (Texas and federal, with California for comparison), EU GDPR, and UK GDPR, as of November 2026. Research method: web search snippets only. Direct page fetches failed because texasattorneygeneral.gov and oag.ca.gov are outside this session's allowed network domains, so most citations are search-indexed pages (law-firm commentary, statute mirrors, policy copies) rather than full primary texts read end to end. Quotes come from search snippets. Check against primary texts before relying on any of them.

## Right of publicity / misappropriation (Texas and general US)

### Takeaway
Texas recognises common-law misappropriation for living people, but the claim needs a use of the name or likeness **for its commercial value**. Incidental and newsworthy uses fall outside it. A free, ad-free planner that names speakers in connection with their public conference activity sits near the low-risk end. The risk rises if a speaker's identity is used to promote the site itself, or if sponsors or ads are added.

### Cited Findings
- **Texas elements (Fifth Circuit):** (1) the defendant appropriated the plaintiff's name or likeness for its value, "not in an incidental manner or for a newsworthy purpose"; (2) the plaintiff can be identified from the publication; (3) the defendant gained some advantage or benefit (one summary truncated this element; confirm the wording in the opinion). — [Matthews v. Wozencraft, 15 F.3d 432 (5th Cir. 1994)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/015/15.F3d.432.93-4434.html); [FindLaw 5th Cir.](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-5th-circuit/1073886.html); [DMLP Texas Right of Publicity guide](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/texas-right-publicity-law)
- Texas protects the commercial value of identity, not a person's life story. — [Matthews v. Wozencraft (Lexplug brief)](https://www.lexplug.com/casebrief/matthews_v_wozencraft_67e33b2c1e9931e3132296bd)
- Newsworthiness is described as the major limit on the tort and is applied generously. It does not cover use for advertising or business purposes. — [DMLP Texas guide](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/texas-right-publicity-law); [Texas Governor's Office, Music Office: misappropriation & right of publicity](https://gov.texas.gov/music/page/misappropriation_and_right_of_publicity)
- A Texas appellate court reportedly allowed a misappropriation claim even for a public figure. — [rightofpublicityroadmap.com Texas page](https://rightofpublicityroadmap.com/state_page/texas/) (secondary; case not verified)
- Texas Property Code ch. 26 is a separate **post-mortem** statutory right. Living people rely on the common-law claim. — [DMLP Texas guide](https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/texas-right-publicity-law); [Promise Legal: Texas publicity rights at a glance](https://blog.promise.legal/startup-central/texas-quick-facts-publicity-rights-at-a-glance/)
- **General US (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652C):** the appropriation tort can reach non-commercial uses "for his own purposes and benefit." But comment d says "a plaintiff's name is not appropriated by mere mention of it," and use in connection with a person's public activities is treated as incidental or privileged. — [Harvard Berkman: Restatement privacy sections](https://cyber.harvard.edu/privacy/Privacy_R2d_Torts_Sections.htm); [DMLP D.C. right of publicity](https://www.dmlp.org/node/15677)
- Courts judge incidental use by "the role that the use of the plaintiff's name or likeness plays in the main purpose and subject of the work." In *Yeager v. Cingular*, use tied to the defendant's marketing was **not** incidental. — [Loeb & Loeb: Yeager v. Cingular Wireless](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2010/01/yeager-v-cingular-wireless-llc-et-al)
- Some states limit the tort to commercial uses by statute. — [Harvard Berkman Restatement page](https://cyber.harvard.edu/privacy/Privacy_R2d_Torts_Sections.htm)
- One unverified source claims a 2025 Texas CPRC ch. 98C on social-media impersonation overlaps with misappropriation. — search snippet via [rulesofcivilprocedure.com](https://rulesofcivilprocedure.com/causes-of-action/tx/invasion-of-privacy/) (low confidence)

### Inferences
- Profiles of announced speakers, describing their public professional role at a public event on a free site with no ads, fit the "newsworthy / incidental / public activities" framing well.
- Risk factors to avoid: using a speaker's photo or name in promotion of the site (social cards that say "X uses our planner"), implying endorsement, adding sponsors or ads next to profiles, or selling or licensing the profile data.
- "Fun facts" that drift into private life make the newsworthiness argument weaker, because they are no longer tied to the public activity.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full Texas appellate opinions or any 2025–2026 Texas statutory changes on likeness or digital replicas.
- I found no case on community-run conference guides specifically.

## Copyright of bios and headshots

### Takeaway
Headshots are usually owned by the **photographer**. The speaker or conference typically holds only a licence, and the licence may or may not cover third-party sites. Bios are copyrightable expression (usually the speaker's), but their facts are free to use. Safest approach: write short bios in your own words, or quote briefly with attribution and a link. Show headshots only via the official source (hotlink or embed) or with permission, and offer removal.

### Cited Findings
- The photographer owns the image; clients typically get "a limited license to use the image." — [Businesses Grow: Event photos and the law](https://businessesgrow.com/2015/04/08/event-photos-law)
- Photos of a speaker stay with the photographer unless copyright is transferred in writing. — [Speakers Canada: copyright of images/videos](https://speakerscanada.com/copyright-images-videos/); [Meetings & Conventions: Who owns intellectual property?](https://www.meetings-conventions.com/Resources/Legal/WHO-OWNS-INTELLECTUAL-PROPERTY-)
- Conferences sometimes use a formal "Speaker Headshot Image Licence," which shows that rights in headshots are treated as something to license. — [ITEC speaker headshot image licence](https://www.itec.co.uk/speaker-headshot-image-licence); [DSEI speaker headshot image licence](https://www.dsei.co.uk/speaker-headshot-image-licence)
- Typical speaker agreements grant the **organiser** (not third parties) rights in the speaker's name, likeness, photo and bio. They range from broad ("all media and in all territories", Euromoney) to promotion-only ("to promote the event", template). Some speakers reserve rights beyond event promotion. — [Euromoney Speaker Agreement Standard Terms](https://euromoney.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Speaker-Agreement-Standard-Terms.pdf); [AI Lawyer speaker agreement template](https://ailawyer.pro/templates/speaker-agreement-template); [internetcases.com: IP issues in a speaker's agreement](https://internetcases.com/?p=5900); [WSO2Con 2026 terms](https://wso2.com/wso2con/2026/africa/cfp/terms-conditions)
- Facts are not protected; expression is. Factual use is more likely fair; biographies have often been given fair-use latitude, but not always (*Salinger*). — [LMU library: copyright & fair use](https://library.lmunet.edu/ILT/copyright_fairuse); [Copyright Office summary: Rosemont v. Random House](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/rosemontenters-randomhouse-2dcir1966.pdf); [UVA: fair use and biography](https://thetaper.library.virginia.edu/2020/02/28/fair-use-week-day-5-fair-use-and-biography-a-first-draft-interview.html)
- **Hotlinking / embedding:** the Ninth Circuit's "server test" (*Perfect 10 v. Amazon*, 2007; reaffirmed in *Hunley v. Instagram*, 2023) says a site that embeds an image hosted elsewhere does not "display" it, so it is not directly liable. Other courts are split: several district courts outside the Ninth Circuit have rejected it, and no other circuit has explicitly adopted it. The **Fifth Circuit (Texas)** may take up the question. — [Davis Wright Tremaine: Hunley v. Instagram](https://dwt.com/insights/2023/08/server-test-instagram-case-ninth-circuit); [Finnegan: server test survives](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/embedding-and-the-server-test-survive-in-the-ninth-circuitfor-now.html); [Mondaq: Fifth Circuit may decide embedding](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/copyright/1805848/is-embedding-someone-elses-image-copyright-infringement-the-fifth-circuit-may-finally-tell-us)
- Copying the image to your own server weakens the server-test defence (*Evox v. Verizon Media*). — [Loeb & Loeb: Evox v. Verizon Media](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2022/12/evox-productions-llc-v-verizon-media-inc)

### Inferences
- For a Texas-based owner, the server test is **not settled law** in the Fifth Circuit. Hotlinking reduces risk but does not remove it. Note also that hotlinking leaks visitors' IP addresses to the image host, and it breaks if the host changes URLs.
- Copying ~273 headshots into the site's own static assets is the higher-risk choice for copyright. The headshots were licensed to AfroTech, not to the site, and the photographers are unknown.
- Best practice: credit "Photo and bio: AfroTech 2026 official schedule" with a link; prefer original short summaries over full bio copies; for researched content, write original sentences and link to sources rather than copying article text; honour any takedown request at once (DMCA-style).
- Researched "recent articles/talks" should be links with titles, not copied excerpts or thumbnails.

### Gaps
- I could not find AfroTech's or Sessionboard's actual speaker terms or API/data terms. The owner should check the official schedule's terms of use and, ideally, ask AfroTech for permission.
- I did not verify whether the Fifth Circuit has ruled on embedding as of Nov 2026.

## Defamation / false light from inaccurate "facts"

### Takeaway
In Texas, a private-figure speaker need only show **negligence** as to truth. Most speakers are probably private or limited-purpose public figures, so careless "fun facts" carry real exposure. Texas's Defamation Mitigation Act strongly rewards a fast, visible correction process. Careful sourcing is the best evidence that you were not negligent.

### Cited Findings
- Texas fault standard: negligence for private figures; actual malice for public officials/figures. — [rulesofcivilprocedure.com: Texas defamation](https://rulesofcivilprocedure.com/causes-of-action/tx/defamation/); [Nolo: Texas defamation](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/how-long-do-i-have-to-file-a-defamation-lawsuit-in-texas.html)
- **Defamation Mitigation Act** (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §§ 73.051–.062): a plaintiff "may maintain an action for defamation only if" they made a timely, sufficient written request for correction, clarification or retraction (identifying the statement with particularity), or the defendant has already corrected or retracted. Failing to request one within 90 days of learning of the publication bars exemplary damages. — [HB 1759 bill analysis (83R)](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/83R/analysis/html/HB01759E.htm); [HB 1759 enrolled text](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/83R/billtext/html/HB01759F.htm); [Tex. CPRC ch. 73](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/CP/pdf/CP.73.pdf)
- The Texas Supreme Court read the request requirement as mandatory (*Hogan v. Zoanni*, 2020). — [Texas Supreme Court opinion 18-0944](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1452311/180944d.pdf)
- Limitations period: generally one year. — [Nolo](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/how-long-do-i-have-to-file-a-defamation-lawsuit-in-texas.html)
- The Texas Citizens Participation Act (anti-SLAPP) may apply to defamation claims (not verified in detail). — search context via [Courthouse News: Jones v. TMZ](https://www.courthousenews.com/former-nfl-stars-suit-against-tmz-allowed-to-move-forward/)

### Inferences
- Every researched fact should carry a link to a reliable source (the speaker's own site or LinkedIn, an employer page, a reputable outlet), plus a "verified on" date. Unsourced or single-weak-source claims should not be published.
- Mixing up two people with the same name is the classic failure mode for 273 profiles. Match on name **plus** company or title before attributing an article or "fun fact."
- A published correction policy, a visible correction note, and quick turnaround fit how the Texas Act works. They also show the reasonable care that answers a negligence claim.
- False light: Texas does not recognise false light as a separate tort (from my background knowledge, not verified this session; see Gaps). Other states do, so accuracy matters for non-Texas speakers too.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm Texas's rejection of false light (*Cain v. Hearst*, 1994) this session. It is from training knowledge.
- I did not pin down how courts would classify conference speakers as public or private figures.

## Privacy law: GDPR, UK GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, TDPSA

### Takeaway
**US:** CCPA does not apply (it covers for-profit businesses above thresholds). TDPSA excludes SBA-defined small businesses and "publicly available information." A one-person, non-commercial site is very likely out of scope of both. Texas's data-broker law targets businesses that sell data, so it likely does not apply either. **EU/UK:** GDPR probably does not reach a US site that merely is accessible from Europe and does not target EU residents (Art. 3(2)). If it did apply, the realistic routes are **legitimate interests** plus Article 14 notice (or the disproportionate-effort exception) plus an easy objection right, or the **journalism** exemption, which the CJEU reads broadly (*Buivids*).

### Cited Findings
**CCPA/CPRA (California)**
- Applies to for-profit entities doing business in California that meet a threshold such as >$25M gross revenue; non-profits are generally outside scope because they do not operate "for the profit or financial benefit" of owners. — [CPPA FAQ](https://cppa.ca.gov/faq.html); [IAPP: CPRA defining "business"](https://iapp.org/news/a/cpras-top-operational-impacts-part-2-defining-business); [TrueVault: when CCPA doesn't apply](https://safe.truevault.com/learn/ccpa/when-ccpa-doesnt-apply)
- "Publicly available" information is excluded from personal information. That includes information a business reasonably believes the consumer lawfully made available to the general public, or that appears in widely distributed media. Pending SB 435 (2025–26) would narrow this; its status was not verified. — [CPPA FAQ](https://cppa.ca.gov/faq.html); [LegiScan CA SB 435](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB435/2025)

**TDPSA (Texas)**
- Small businesses as defined by the SBA are generally exempt, except they must get consent before **selling** sensitive data. Texas was the first state to include this exception. — [O'Melveny](https://www.omm.com/insights/alerts-publications/signed-texas-added-to-the-list-of-states-enacting-a-comprehensive-privacy-law/); [Davis Wright Tremaine overview](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2023/07/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act-overview); [Texas State Law Library](https://sll.texas.gov/spotlight/2024/07/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act)
- "Publicly available information" is excluded from "personal data." That covers information lawfully made available through widely distributed media, by the consumer, or by someone the consumer disclosed it to, unless the consumer restricted it to a specific audience. — [Baker Hostetler overview](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/privacy-deep-in-the-heart-of-texas-an-overview-of-the-texas-data-privacy-and-security-act); [Troutman (Locke Lord) QuickStudy](https://www.troutman.com/insights/locke-lord-quickstudy-texas-data-privacy-and-security-act/); [Texas AG TDPSA page](https://texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/consumer-privacy-rights/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act) (could not fetch)

**Texas data-broker law (Bus. & Com. Code ch. 509)**
- Applies to business entities that collect or transfer personal data they did not collect directly from the individual. As amended effective Sept 1, 2025 (SB 2121, SB 1343), it requires annual registration with the Secretary of State. Penalty: $100/day, capped at $10,000 per 12 months. The original law had a revenue-based test (data on >50,000 individuals). — [WilmerHale: Texas expands data broker law](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250904-texas-expands-and-modifies-data-broker-registration-law); [Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 509.005](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._&_com._code_section_509.005); [SB 2105 analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/analysis/html/SB02105F.htm)

**GDPR territorial scope (EU)**
- "Mere accessibility" of a website in the EU is not targeting. Art. 3(2) needs intentional offering of goods or services to, or monitoring of, people in the EU. Without targeting, processing EU residents' data is not by itself enough. — [Faegre Drinker on EDPB Guidelines 3/2018](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2018/12/in-or-out-edpb-publishes-much-awaited-guidance-on-gdprs-territorial-scope); [A&L Goodbody: finalised territorial scope guidelines](https://www.algoodbody.com/insights-publications/edpb-publishes-finalised-guidelines-on-territorial-scope-of-the-gdpr); [datarequests.org](https://www.datarequests.org/blog/gdpr-territorial-scope/)
- If Art. 3(2) applies and the controller has no EU establishment, a representative is generally required (Art. 27), with exemptions under Art. 27(2). — [Baker McKenzie client alert](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/-/media/files/insight/publications/2019/12/baker-mckenzie-client-alert-edpb--guidlines-on-theterritorial-scope-of-the-gdpr-art3-and-on-representatives-art27--now-adopted-after-public-consultation.pdf?la=en)

**Lawful basis, Article 14, objection (EU/UK)**
- Where legitimate interests is the basis, the interests must be stated in the Art. 13/14 notice (Art. 14(2)(b)), and the right to object must be flagged at first communication (Art. 14(2)(c)). — [Bird & Bird GDPR guide: legitimate interests](https://twobirds.com/-/media/pdfs/gdpr-pdfs/23--guide-to-the-gdpr--legitimate-interests.pdf)
- Art. 14(5)(b) and Recital 62 keep a "disproportionate effort" exception to the Art. 14 notice duty. — [Cooley: handling SARs (UK perspective)](https://www.cooley.com/-/media/cooley/pdf/reprints/2018/handling-sars-now-and-after-may-a-uk-perspective--ann-bevitt-and-nihara-mccormack-privacy--data-protection.pdf)
- Right to object (Art. 21): applies to processing based on legitimate interests; it is not absolute, but the controller must show compelling grounds to keep going. — [ICO: right to object](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-object); [rgpd.com Art. 21](https://rgpd.com/gdpr/chapter-3-rights-of-the-data-subject/article-21-right-to-object)

**Journalism exemption (EU/UK)**
- Art. 85 leaves the scope to each Member State. — [Taylor Wessing: Art. 85 UK GDPR](https://globaldatahub.taylorwessing.com/article/the-journalistic-exemption-understanding-article-85-uk-gdpr); [rgpd.com Art. 85](https://rgpd.com/gdpr/chapter-9-provisions-relating-to-specific-processing-situations/article-85-processing-and-freedom-of-expression-and-information)
- *Buivids* (C-345/17, 2019): "journalistic activities" means disclosing "information, opinions or ideas" to the public through any medium. The exemption covers "every person engaged in journalism," not just media firms, but not material posted merely to expose someone's private activities. — [Cuatrecasas](https://www.cuatrecasas.com/en/global/intellectual-property/art/data-processing-for-journalistic-purposes-judgment-of-the-cjeu); [Inforrm (Erdos)](https://inforrm.org/2019/02/21/european-data-protection-and-freedom-of-expression-after-buivids-an-increasingly-significant-tension-part-one-the-decision-david-erdos/); [FRA case summary](https://fra.europa.eu/pl/caselaw-reference/cjeu-case-c-34517-judgment)
- UK DPA 2018 Sch. 2 Part 5 (the "special purposes" exemption) can disapply Art. 14(1)–(4). — [legislation.gov.uk DPA 2018 Sch. 2 Pt. 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/schedule/2/part/5/enacted); [ICO draft journalism code reference notes](https://cy.ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/4021637/ico-draft-journalism-code-supporting-reference-notes-21092022.pdf)
- A reported 9 July 2026 CJEU judgment held that Art. 85(2) exhaustively lists which provisions Member States may derogate from for journalism (not verified against the judgment). — [ICTRecht case-law blog, July 2026](https://www.ictrecht.nl/en/blog/case-law-blog-data-privacy-july-2026)

### Inferences
- **US:** practically out of scope of CCPA, TDPSA and Texas data-broker registration, as long as the site stays non-commercial and **never sells or licenses** the compiled dataset. Selling it would be the act most likely to bring the data-broker law into play.
- **EU/UK:** the site probably falls outside GDPR (no targeting). Still, the cheap way to be safe is to act as if legitimate interests applies: keep data minimal and tied to the speaker's public role, publish a short privacy notice for speakers (what, sources, why, how to object), and **honour every objection by removal** rather than arguing "compelling grounds." That covers Art. 14 and Art. 21 in spirit at low cost. Proactively emailing each speaker may count as disproportionate effort, though notifying via AfroTech or a public notice is a good middle path.
- Relying on the journalism exemption is a weaker fit for a planner directory than for reporting, and it varies by country. Treat it as a backup, not the primary basis.

- I did not find EDPB-specific guidance on Art. 14 for web-scraped public profiles. The Art. 29 WP transparency guidelines (WP260) were not retrieved.

## Sensitive data categories and data-broker-style compilation

### Takeaway
Never publish sensitive or special-category data, even if the speaker posted it somewhere. Avoid assembling scattered public facts into a dossier. Compiling and **selling** such profiles is what data-broker laws target, and aggregation itself raises the privacy harm.

### Cited Findings
- TDPSA's small-business carve-out still requires consent before **selling** sensitive data. That shows the legislature treats sensitive data as the core concern. — [O'Melveny](https://www.omm.com/insights/alerts-publications/signed-texas-added-to-the-list-of-states-enacting-a-comprehensive-privacy-law/)
- The TDPSA public-information exclusion does not cover information the consumer restricted to a specific audience (e.g. a private or friends-only post). — [Baker Hostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/privacy-deep-in-the-heart-of-texas-an-overview-of-the-texas-data-privacy-and-security-act)
- The Texas data-broker law targets entities handling personal data they did not collect directly from the individual. — [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250904-texas-expands-and-modifies-data-broker-registration-law)
- SPJ: "Realize that private people have a greater right to control information about themselves than public figures"; weigh the consequences of publishing personal information; only an overriding public need justifies intrusion. — [SPJ Code via University of Hawaii](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~jour/spj/SPJcode.html); [SPJ Code (Notre Dame PDF)](https://studentmedia.nd.edu/assets/330169/spj_code_of_ethics.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Never publish:** health or disability; religion; political opinions or affiliations (beyond a professional role the speaker publicly holds); sexual orientation or gender identity (unless in the official bio, and even then only as written); race/ethnicity labels beyond the official bio; immigration status; criminal history; union membership; family members, children, partners; home address, neighbourhood, phone, personal email; date of birth or age; school-age details; salary or finances; precise location or travel plans; personal social accounts (non-professional); speculation of any kind.
- GDPR Art. 9 special categories (health, religion, politics, sex life/orientation, ethnicity, union membership, biometrics, genetics) match this list. Note that headshots are not biometric data unless processed for identification. Do not run face matching to find or verify people.
- "Fun facts" are the highest-risk field. Restrict them to things the speaker has **published about themselves in a professional context**, such as a hobby in their own bio or a talk topic.
- No bulk export, API or downloadable dataset of the profiles. Do not let search engines index a "people" dataset beyond the profile pages (consider `noindex` for the researched fields, or for the whole profile pages).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the TDPSA's statutory list of "sensitive data" or the GDPR Art. 9 text this session. The category lists above come from background knowledge.

## Best practices: SPJ Code, Wikipedia BLP, community platforms

### Takeaway
Both journalism ethics and Wikipedia's BLP policy point the same way: source everything conservatively, remove contested or poorly sourced material **immediately** without waiting for debate, put the burden of proof on whoever adds a claim, and correct mistakes promptly and visibly.

### Cited Findings
- **Wikipedia BLP:** "Contentious material about living persons … that is unsourced or poorly sourced—whether the material is negative, positive, neutral, or just questionable—must be removed immediately." — [Wikipedia: Biographies of living persons](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP:BLP)
- BLP: the burden of evidence rests on whoever adds or restores material, especially for living persons. When in doubt, cut back to a version that is fully sourced, neutral and on-topic. Removed content may return only if it conforms to policy. — [Wikipedia: BLP](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP:BLP); [Simple English Wikipedia: BLP](https://simple.wikipedia.com/wiki/Wikipedia:BLP)
- BLP allows self-published material by the subject about themselves if it is "not unduly self-serving." — [The Register (2008) on BLP](https://www.theregister.com/2008/02/06/the_cult_of_wikipedia/?page=4)
- **SPJ:** "Acknowledge mistakes and correct them promptly and prominently. Explain corrections and clarifications carefully and clearly." Balance the public's need for information against potential harm. The code is a guide, not legally enforceable. — [SPJ Code (Notre Dame PDF)](https://studentmedia.nd.edu/assets/330169/spj_code_of_ethics.pdf); [InvestigateWest copy](https://www.investigatewest.org/spjcodeofethics/); [UTEP copy](https://www.utep.edu/student-affairs/student-publications/publications/journalism-ethics1/journalism-ethics.html)
- Newsroom correction policies built on SPJ are an example of the published-policy approach. — [San Antonio Report ethics policy](https://sanantonioreport.org/ethics-policy/); [Monterey County Now ethics & corrections](https://www.montereycountynow.com/site/ethics_and_corrections_policies.html)

### Inferences
- Adopt a BLP-style rule: any speaker-raised concern removes the disputed field first, then you discuss. Do not leave it up while you check.
- Turnaround target: remove disputed content within 24–48 hours of a request; acknowledge the request within the same window. This is my suggestion, not a sourced standard. During the conference week, aim for same day.
- Log sources: for each researched field, store the source URL, retrieval date, and who added it (git history already provides an audit trail). Keep a changelog of corrections; show a "corrected on" note on the profile for substantive fixes, but not for removals the speaker asked to keep quiet.

## Practical guardrails and request-a-change wording

### Takeaway
Publish only professional, public-role information with a source link next to each item. Never publish private or sensitive data. Do not copy headshots to your own server without permission. Offer a one-click, no-questions-asked removal or correction route, and apply it quickly.

### Cited Findings
- The Texas Defamation Mitigation Act treats a written correction request as the gateway to suit and rewards correction. A clear request channel helps both sides. — [HB 1759 analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/83R/analysis/html/HB01759E.htm)
- GDPR/UK GDPR: tell people about the right to object at first communication; if you rely on legitimate interests, say so. — [Bird & Bird](https://twobirds.com/-/media/pdfs/gdpr-pdfs/23--guide-to-the-gdpr--legitimate-interests.pdf); [ICO right to object](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-object)
- Wikipedia BLP: remove first, then discuss. — [Wikipedia: BLP](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP:BLP)
- The project's own rules (CLAUDE.md) already say to keep organisers' personal contact details off the site and to credit sources; the same principle extends to speakers. — /home/user/afrotech/CLAUDE.md

### Inferences
**Publish (with source link next to each item):**
- Name, title, company, and session(s) exactly as in the official schedule; credit "From the AfroTech 2026 official schedule."
- Official bio: quote as-is with credit, or a short original summary.
- Headshot: hotlink from the official source with credit, or use initials/avatar placeholder; copy only with permission.
- Professional website and LinkedIn **only** if the speaker publicly lists them, or if they are clearly the same person (match on name plus company).
- Recent talks, articles, podcasts **authored by or featuring the speaker** in a professional capacity: title, outlet, date, link.
- "Fun facts" only from the speaker's own public professional statements, phrased neutrally, with a link.

**Never publish:** the sensitive list above, plus anything the speaker restricted to a specific audience; opinion or evaluation of the speaker; anything from a source you cannot link; AI-generated claims without a human-verified source; content about family or relationships; anything that suggests an endorsement of the site.

**Process:**
- A label on each profile: "Researched by volunteers from public sources. Last checked {date}."
- A "Request a change or removal" link on every profile, using a GitHub issue form *or* an email address or form for people without GitHub accounts. Do not require an account, proof of identity for simple removals, or a reason.
- Remove on request within 48 hours (same day during Nov 2–6), with a reply. Corrections show a short "Updated {date}" note.
- Suppression list: a removed speaker or field stays removed when scripts re-run. The build scripts regenerate data, so the opt-out list must be an input the scripts respect, not a hand edit to generated files.
- Speaker notice: a short "About speaker profiles" page explaining sources, purpose (helping attendees plan), no sale or sharing, and how to opt out. Optionally ask AfroTech to share it with speakers.
- Retention: take researched fields down or archive them after the event (e.g. 30–90 days), keeping only what's in the official schedule.

**Sample wording (draft):**
> **Is this you?** We build these profiles from AfroTech's official schedule and from public sources linked on this page, to help attendees find your sessions. If anything is wrong, out of date, or you'd rather it not be here, tell us and we'll fix or remove it, usually within two days and the same day during conference week. You don't need to give a reason. [Request a change] · [Remove my profile]

### Gaps
- Turnaround times and retention periods are proposals. I found no binding standard for a site like this.
- No sourced guidance on whether hotlinked official headshots would be acceptable to AfroTech or Sessionboard; asking them directly would settle it.
